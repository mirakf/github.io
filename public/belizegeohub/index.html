BelizeGeoHub#

An Open Ecosystem Mapping Framework for Belize#

Belize holds some of the most biodiverse and ecologically critical landscapes in the Western Hemisphere — and until now, no one had a current map of them. BelizeGeoHub is an open, annually updatable ecosystem mapping framework built to change that.

🗺️ Why This Map Needed to Exist#

Belize has experienced accelerating urbanization, agricultural expansion, and climate-driven environmental change over the past decade. Yet the country’s most recent ecosystem map dated to 2017 — nearly a decade behind the pace of change on the ground. Field-based mapping is too slow and too costly to keep up, leaving policymakers, conservation organizations, and Belizean communities without current data for the decisions that matter most.

BelizeGeoHub was built to close that gap. Using satellite remote sensing, Google Earth Engine, and machine learning, we developed a reproducible framework that can be updated annually as new data become available — providing a low-cost, high-accuracy alternative to traditional field inventories. All outputs are openly available for download, giving Belizean agencies, researchers, and conservation organizations direct access to current ecosystem data.

🌍 Explore the App#

The BelizeGeoHub web application lets you explore Belize’s 27 ecosystem types interactively across the full 2015–2026 time series. Toggle between years to visualize how the landscape has changed, zoom into specific regions, and compare ecosystem distributions across the country.

🔭 What Is Coming Next#

BelizeGeoHub is a living project. The next major update will add ecosystem service layers to the existing framework, allowing users to explore not just where ecosystems are, but what they provide — from carbon storage and coastal protection to biodiversity support and water regulation.

Planned additions include:

  • 🌿 Carbon Storage & Sequestration
  • 🌊 Coastal Protection & Wave Attenuation
  • 💧 Water Quality Regulation
  • 🐦 Biodiversity & Habitat Quality
  • 🏙️ Nature-Based Solutions Planning Layers

If you are a researcher, conservation organization, or government agency interested in collaborating on or contributing to these layers, get in touch!

⚙️ How It Was Built#

The Challenge of Ecosystem Mapping at Scale#

Mapping 27 distinct ecosystem types across an entire country — from coral reefs and seagrass beds to submontane pine forests and mangrove littorals — is not a problem you can solve by hand. The scale, the complexity, and the need for annual updates demanded a machine learning approach grounded in the best available remote sensing data.

Variable Selection and Modeling#

From an initial pool of 290 GIS-derived and remotely sensed variables, we applied a variable importance function within an eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model — implemented in the R caret package — to identify the ten most informative predictors:

  • 🏔️ Slope and elevation
  • 👥 Human population count
  • 🌧️ Precipitation
  • 🌊 Bathymetry
  • 🗺️ Two prior Belize ecosystem maps
  • 📡 Sentinel-1 Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) incidence angle
  • 🌡️ Landsat-8 thermal infrared (Band 10)
  • 🌿 Gray-Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) texture measure derived from Landsat-8 Band 5

The classification was run entirely in Google Earth Engine, achieving an overall accuracy of 82.0%, rising to 87.3% after post-classification refinements including marine-terrestrial separation using the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL).

The full codebase is openly available on GitHub so that other researchers and tropical countries facing similar mapping gaps can adapt this framework for their own contexts.

🌿 Ground Truth: Boots on the Ground#

Numbers and algorithms only go so far. In February 2026, a team from Boston University joined forces with Wild Earth Allies to ground-truth the classification across Central Belize — hiking into submontane forests, wading through mangroves, and confirming ecosystem boundaries with some of the most experienced botanists and ecologists working in Belize today.

Using a stratified random sampling design, the team confirmed 12 distinct forest types across both lowland and submontane environments, including mangrove, littoral, scrub, moist, pine, and wet forests. The validation data from this expedition directly informed the post-classification adjustments that pushed overall accuracy to 87.3%.
